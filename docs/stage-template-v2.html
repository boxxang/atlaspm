Silicon Program Stage Template

The current template carries twelve stages — the path the design database travels. What actually breaks schedules sits beside that path: IP that is not silicon-proven on the chosen node, a PDK still at 0.5, a compiler SRAM that misses its density budget, a probe card with a twenty-week lead time, a package nobody stressed until the real dies arrived. Eleven of those workstreams are proposed here as stages of their own, and the implementation stages are rebuilt around how work actually flows — netlists arrive as a sequence of drops, mask data leaves in two releases a month apart. Every activity carries an elapsed TAT and an effort estimate in man-months.

Schedule anchors · why 132 weeks

An earlier draft of this template ran 92 weeks. Checked against what leading-edge programs actually report, the back half held up and the front half did not — RTL development at fifteen weeks and verification closure at eighteen were not schedules, they were wishes. The anchors below are the check. Ranges are for a new architecture on a leading node with 2.5D packaging and HBM, not a derivative; a derivative of an existing design runs roughly 40 % shorter and is a separate profile, not this one.

How to give feedback. Every activity and deliverable has a stable ID — IPR-03, PKGD-D6. Point at the ID and say cut, move, rename, or change the TAT or the M/M. Stage-level calls: keep, drop, merge with another, or split.

TAT is elapsed calendar time — how long the activity occupies the schedule, not how much work it contains. cont. marks an activity that runs continuously across the stage rather than closing once. M/M is effort in man-months. The two are independent: a four-week foundry contract negotiation costs two man-months, while a four-week timing closure sprint costs forty. Dividing M/M by TAT in months gives the headcount that activity needs while it is live — useful for spotting where the plan quietly assumes twenty engineers who do not exist.

The two vertical rules on the map are hard gates. Week 78 — signoff closed and chip-package co-verification (SIPI) signed off, the precondition for releasing any mask data. Week 98 — package validation on test vehicles (PTV) complete before product wafers leave the fab, so the first real assembly runs into a frozen process window rather than an open question.

Mask release is two events, not one. The diamonds inside the Tapeout bar mark FEOL MTO at week 80 and BEOL MTO at week 84. The four weeks between them are the BEOL fix window — metal-layer ECOs land there while the front-end mask set is already being cut, and wafer start waits only on the FEOL masks.

Synthesis hands over four times, not once. N0 is the flow-flush netlist that lets physical design build its flow; N1 and N2 are quality turns; the FFN is the only netlist the final turn sees. SYN and PD therefore overlap by twenty weeks rather than meeting at a single handoff date.

Weeks are offsets from program kickoff; the template stays relative so every program's dates fall out of its own kickoff date. Effort figures are for a large 4 nm-class accelerator with 2.5D packaging and two HBM stacks, excluding software, firmware and SDK — those sit outside this template's scope.
